The Pico 2 Laboratory

A Raspberry Pi Pico 2 in the browser: write a program in RISC-V assembly or in a subset of C, run it on a simulated RP2350 with its Hazard3 cores, and watch the instructions, the clock cycles and the pins.

About the laboratory

The Raspberry Pi Pico 2 is a small board built around the RP2350, a microcontroller from Raspberry Pi Ltd. The RP2350 has two processor cores, and each can run either as an Arm Cortex-M33 or as a Hazard3, an open-source RISC-V core; the choice is made at boot (RP2350 Datasheet §3.8, §3.9). The laboratory simulates the RISC-V side: two Hazard3 cores, the RP2350's address map (§2.2) and the devices listed under The chip's devices.

It is a teaching tool. A program runs one instruction at a time or at the speed of the real chip; every instruction takes the clock cycles the datasheet gives for Hazard3, and the Core view shows why. Programs are written in Hazard3 assembly language or in MAVILERA C, a teaching subset of C compiled in the page. What the model leaves out is listed under Limits of the model, and how it was checked under How the models were checked.

Getting started

  1. Open a template from Templates in the toolbar, for example Hello over UART. Its first lines say what it shows and what to try.
  2. Choose Assemble (Compile for C), or press Ctrl+Enter. Errors are underlined in the editor and listed in the console.
  3. Step (F10) runs one instruction; Step back (Shift+F10) undoes it. Run (F5) runs on and pauses.
  4. Open the Pins tab of the bottom pane to see GP25, the board's LED, and the other 29 GPIOs.

A path through the templates: A tour of Hazard3's extensions; Hello over UART and Blink GP25 with the SIO and TIMER0; Blink in C and Hello in C; A button on GP15 counts presses and Echo over UART in capitals; then the pipeline, sound, two cores, the buses and PIO. The practice exercises follow the same order.

Assembly language

The assembler takes Hazard3's instruction set: RV32IMAC with the bit-manipulation and code-size extensions, and Hazard3's own extensions, among them its interrupt controller (RP2350 Datasheet §3.8, §3.8.6). A program begins at its first instruction in .text; its data go in .data; it ends at ebreak when no trap handler is set. Without a bootrom (see Limits) the program itself starts the chip: it starts the 12 MHz crystal, chooses the clocks and takes the devices it uses out of reset, as the device templates do.

clk_sys in the toolbar chooses 1, 4 or 12 MHz. The assembler defines symbols for that choice, so that a program works at every clock:

SymbolMeaning
CLK_SYS_DIVThe value for CLK_SYS_DIV that divides the 12 MHz reference to clk_sys (§8.1).
CLK_SYS_HZclk_sys in hertz.
UART_IBRD, UART_FBRDThe UART's baud-rate divisors for this clock (§12.1).
TICK_CYCLESThe tick generator's count for a 1 µs timer tick (§8.5).

A .pio file in the second tab (File, then Add PIO file) is assembled with the program; a PIO program named blink gives the symbols blink_program (its words), blink_length, blink_wrap and blink_wrap_target.

MAVILERA C

MAVILERA C is the teaching subset of C of the MAVILERA laboratories, compiled in the page into Hazard3 assembly that you can read and step through. Its start-up starts the crystal, sets clk_sys to the chosen clock, starts UART0 for putchar and the timer, then calls main; main's return value is the program's exit code (in a0 at the end). Step line and Back line run one C line.

#include <rp2350.h> gives the registers by name and these helpers:

HelpersWhat they do
pin_output, pin_input, pin_write, pin_readA GPIO as a single-cycle IO output or input.
delay_us, delay_ms, sleep_ms, time_usWaiting and time from TIMER0 (sleep_ms waits asleep, for programs without interrupt handlers).
pwm_start(pin, top, div), pwm_level(pin, level)A pin's PWM slice: a period of top + 1 counts of clk_sys / div, high for level counts.
dma_copy(dst, src, words)A copy by DMA channel 0, waiting until it is done.
adc_read(input)A conversion of input 0 to 3 (GP26 to GP29) or 4 (the temperature sensor): 0 to 4095.
i2c_setup, i2c_write, i2c_readI2C0 on GP4 (SDA) and GP5 (SCL), the lab kit's bus.
spi_setup, spi_select, spi_transferSPI0 on GP16 to GP19, chip select on GP17.
core1_start, fifo_push, fifo_pop, core_idCore 1 runs the program's core1_main; words pass through the inter-core FIFOs.

CLK_SYS_HZ, UART_BAUDThe chosen clock and UART0's baud rate.

MAVILERA C has no interrupt handlers; the interrupt templates are in assembly.

The panel sets the environment the sensors read and the voltages on GP26 to GP29, the ADC's inputs (§12.4); the temperature is also the chip's own, read by its temperature sensor. Every change is an input of the run, given at the clock cycle you make it. The parts answer their buses byte by byte; their measuring times, filters, interrupt pins and the EEPROM's write time are not modelled.

The Core view

Hazard3 has three pipeline stages: F (fetch), X (execute) and M (memory). The view shows them cycle by cycle: which instruction is in each stage, the drawing of the stages, the cycles each instruction cost and the cause of every extra cycle (a load whose result is used at once, a taken branch, a divide, a multi-cycle push or pop), and the branch predictor's single entry. The counts are the datasheet's (RP2350 Datasheet §3.8.7). Where Hazard3's hardware design files take a different count (divide and remainder, mret, cm.popretz with one register), the laboratory keeps the datasheet's and says so in the view's help. The bus is perfect: every access completes without wait states.

PIO

The RP2350 has three PIO blocks of four state machines each (RP2350 Datasheet chapter 11). A state machine runs a program of up to 32 instructions that drives and reads pins with exact timing, with its own clock divider, FIFOs to and from the cores, and side-set. In the laboratory a PIO program is written in a .pio file in the Pico SDK's notation; programs that use the RP2350's additions start with .pio_version 1. The PIO tab shows each state machine's program counter, scratch registers, shift registers, FIFOs and divider.

All three blocks run exact to the clock cycle, with the RP2350's additions. Simulating a state machine that executes an instruction on every cycle of a fast clock is slow (at 150 MHz about 1 to 2 % of real time); one that waits on its divider, a FIFO or a pin runs close to real time.

Sound

Choose a pin as the Speaker in the Pins panel and turn Sound on (the speaker button in the toolbar): you hear that pin's level as a small loudspeaker on it would play it. The Converter is Pin-accurate by default (the pin's exact high and low times, filtered to 20 kHz); Ideal DAC plays a PWM output's duty cycle at each period, without its carrier. Sound plays in Real time; Render and play, in the Speed menu, runs up to 60 seconds of the chip's time as fast as it can and then plays them, for programs too fast for real time.

Two cores

After reset core 1 waits in the bootrom for core 0 to send it a short sequence of words through the inter-core FIFO (RP2350 Datasheet §5.3, §3.1). The laboratory models that wait without running the bootrom. In C, core1_start() sends the sequence and core 1 runs core1_main on a stack of its own. Both cores then run at once, interleaved by their clock cycles; Step steps core 0 while core 1 runs alongside, and the Core 1 registers show the other core.

Real time, speed and replay

SpeedWhat it does
Real time (the default)One second of the chip's time per second, so that blinks, tones and UART lines come as on the board. If this computer cannot keep up, the status says how much of real time it reaches.
Full speedAs fast as this computer can.
AnimatedOne step after another, slowly enough to follow.

A pin you drive, a line you send to UART0 and a change of the lab kit are the run's inputs, each given at a clock cycle. Replay in the Pins panel starts the program again and gives the same inputs at the same cycles: the run repeats exactly. A share link and a settings file carry the inputs with the program.

Circuits on the Board

The Board view draws the circuit around the board: at first the demo circuit (a push button on GP15, a knob on GP26 with its 3.3 V and ground), the board's own LED on GP25, and, when chosen in the Pins panel, the lab kit's modules and a speaker. All of them except the board's LED are parts you can move, re-wire, change or delete, and add to. The circuit is simulated with the program: each pin's voltage is solved from the parts on its net whenever something changes, at that clock cycle, and the chip reads its input from that voltage. Capacitors charge and discharge exactly to the cycle.

Editing. Turn on Edit circuit in the Board's heading (the program cannot run while it is on). Choose a part from the tray (its picture is its symbol; its name is in the tooltip) and click the sheet, or press A. Click a pin to start a wire and click again to bend it; end it on a pin, a pad, a junction or another wire. The tools above the tray select and move, draw wires, delete parts or wires, pan, turn, mirror, undo and redo. Everything can be done from the keyboard: arrow keys move a cursor on the grid, Enter selects, W starts a wire, F2 changes a value in Inspect, and ? lists every key. The Circuit tab lists the same circuit as a table with a menu for each pin's connection; there a part of your own can also be defined from the simple parts (Define a part).

Where the values come from. 5 mm LEDs: Kingbright WP7113 series (red ID, yellow YD, green GD, blue QBC/D, white QWC/D); diodes: Vishay 1N4148 and the 1N5819 Schottky diode; transistor: onsemi BC547B; light sensor: Advanced Photonix PDV-P8001; thermistor: Vishay NTCLE100E3103 (10 kΩ, B 3977 K); 7-segment display: Kingbright SC56-11SRWA and SA56-11SRWA; RGB LED: Kingbright WP154A4SUREQBFZGW. An LED's knee voltage and resistance are read from two points of its datasheet's curve (2 mA and 10 mA, the currents a pin gives). Chosen where no datasheet gives them: a switch's 0.05 Ω, the light sensor's resistance at 10 lux (5.74 kΩ, the middle of the datasheet's 3-11 kΩ) and its 1 MΩ in the dark, the resistances of the 7-segment and the RGB LED's red. The board's own LED keeps generic values, since the board's documentation does not name its part.

Light and temperature for the light sensor and the thermistor are the Pins panel's Environment values (Light in lux, Temperature in °C), inputs of the run that Replay repeats.

Exercises

File, then Exercise…, lists the practice exercises. Each gives a task and a starter program; Run the tests runs your program once per test, from reset at full speed, with the test's inputs (a pin driven at a given time, text sent to UART0) given when the chip's clock reaches them, and shows expected against actual: memory, registers, the exit code, the console, pin levels, and the number, period and high time of a pin's changes within a stated tolerance. No solutions are included. An exercise file from a teacher (format risc-vista-exercise, with "arch": "pico2") opens the same way.

Files and sharing

  • Your work is kept in this browser: after a refresh or a closed tab the program, its .pio file and the settings come back.
  • File, then Copy share link, puts the program, the view, the settings and the run's inputs into a link. They travel in the part after #, which the browser never sends to a server.
  • File, then Save program and settings…, writes a .json file; File, then Open, reads it back, and also program files.
  • Exports: traces of a run (JSON lines, CSV) with each instruction's clock cycles.

Lite and Full

The published laboratory holds one program in assembly language or in MAVILERA C (Pico Lite). A second edition, Pico Full, with projects of several files, C built with the Pico SDK in the browser and the import of SDK-built UF2 and ELF files, is still in development and is not part of the published laboratory.

Offline use and privacy

Every computation runs in your browser. MAVILERA has no account, sends no telemetry and uploads nothing. After a first visit the hosted version also opens without a network connection, and a single-file version can be downloaded for computers that have none.

Limits of the model

Not modelled

  • The Arm Cortex-M33 cores: only the RISC-V cores run.
  • The bootrom: a program starts directly at its first instruction. Of the bootrom's functions only the lookup that the Pico SDK's start-up uses is answered; a call of another one, or a jump into the bootrom, ends the run with a message. Core 1's wait for its launch is modelled without it.
  • Blocks whose registers read as zero or as last written, with a message: HSTX_CTRL, HSTX_FIFO, TRNG, SHA256, OTP, OTP_DATA, OTP_DATA_RAW, GLITCH_DETECTOR, TBMAN, USB_DEVICE_DPRAM, XIP_AUX, CORESIGHT_TRACE: HSTX, the random number generator, SHA-256, the one-time-programmable memory, the glitch detector, the testbench manager, USB's buffer memory, the flash cache's auxiliary port and trace.
  • Blocks whose registers only hold what was written: SYSCFG, IO_QSPI, PADS_QSPI, ACCESSCTRL, BUSCTRL, XIP_CTRL, QMI, ROSC, POWMAN, USB: the flash interface, the ring oscillator, power management, bus priority, access control and the USB controller.
  • The SIO interpolators and the TMDS encoder; the watchdog's countdown.
  • The security features (secure and non-secure states, the access controls' checks).
  • SPI and I2C as slaves; I2C 10-bit addresses, general calls and START bytes (the low 7 bits of the address are sent).
  • PIO's INLINE_OUT_EN, OUT_STICKY and GPIOBASE (the registers keep what was written).
  • A GPIO function without a model (for example HSTX or USB): the pin is not driven. DMA requests from blocks without a model never come.

Modelled with simplifications

  • A perfect bus: every fetch, load and store takes no wait states, so code in flash, the APB peripherals and bus conflicts cost less than on the chip.
  • Instruction timing from the datasheet where Hazard3's hardware design differs (18 cases; see The Core view).
  • DMA's security and memory-protection settings are stored, not enforced.
  • The ADC is an ideal converter; the lab kit's parts are ideal and answer at once.
  • Sound: the converters are models of a loudspeaker's hearing of the pin, not of an audio circuit.

The circuit outside the chip

  • A teaching model, not a circuit simulator such as SPICE: resistors, switches and potentiometers are linear; LEDs, diodes and the NPN transistor are piecewise linear (off, or a fixed voltage behind a resistance; the transistor cut off, active or saturated); there are no inductors, no reverse breakdown, and temperature changes only the thermistor.
  • A pin's output is a source behind its datasheet's typical resistance at each drive strength; the drive does not weaken further under a heavy load, and the pads' protection diodes are not modelled. Pins read their input through the typical switching points.
  • I2C and SPI modules are models at the level of bytes on their bus: their wires carry no edges, their pull-ups only decide whether they answer.
  • The speaker and the piezo buzzer listen to their pin and draw no current; the buzzer's capacitance is not modelled.
  • Part values come from named parts' datasheets (see Circuits on the Board); a few that no datasheet gives are chosen, and graph readings are about ±0.02 V.

Speed and browsers

  • A PIO state machine that executes on every cycle of a fast clock runs far slower than real time.
  • Real time at 12 MHz may not be reached; the status says how much is.
  • Measured on one laptop only (see How the models were checked); Safari was not tested.

What a simulation shows

The models were checked against emulators, test suites and the core's hardware design, not against a Pico 2 board. A simulation shows what the model does; it is not evidence of how a hardware build behaves.
